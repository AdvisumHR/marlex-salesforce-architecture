Ways of workingHow specifications are defined

Before anything is built, each change is first analysed and given a clear decision — keep it, drop it, or reshape it — against the way the business wants to work, not against how today's tool happens to do it. Only then does it move on to be built and released.

Two boards, two jobs. Nothing reaches the build board until it has been analysed and its specification accepted — a board item is first “something to analyse”, not “something to build”.

Two stages, kept separate: decide, then build

The work runs through two distinct stages, each with its own owner. The first is an analysis stage, owned by the product side: every item from the functional inventory — and the parts of today's tool that only exist as vendor functionality with nothing to inspect — is walked through and given a disposition. Keep it as it is, drop it, or merge it into something else. The output of that stage is an agreed specification.

The second stage is build and release, owned by the delivery team: an accepted specification is taken through build, testing, business validation and go-live. The two are deliberately not merged into one queue — the mistake to avoid is letting a not-yet-analysed idea drop straight onto the build list. Something on the board is first 'something to analyse', and only becomes 'something to build' once the analysis is done.

The yardstick: fill vacancies faster

Every decision is measured against a single business objective — put the right candidate into the vacancy as quickly and cost-effectively as possible, and cover more vacancies faster. A change earns its place only if it moves that objective; otherwise it is dropped rather than rebuilt out of habit.

Recruitment is treated as a process with two kinds of step. The hygienic steps — data entry, generating reports, enrolments, parsing CVs — are pure friction and should be automated away. The human steps — the relationship with the candidate and the client, the judgement of fit — are the real value and must not be automated. The objective is also the filter that stops the target design inflating: an improvement that does not speed up filling vacancies is not an improvement, it is scope creep.

Current-state as a checklist, target-state as the lens

Each item is decided by walking the current way of working and judging every piece against the target way of working. The current state is not what gets rebuilt — it is the coverage checklist that stops anything being silently forgotten. The target state is not invented freely — it is the lens each 'does this still make sense?' decision is made against.

Rebuilding a piece unchanged should be the exception that has to justify itself, not the default. If something is kept as-is, that must be a conscious 'it still fits' decision, recorded as such — not inertia. The target process is captured once at the level of a whole capability, so the individual decisions stay coherent with it rather than becoming a patchwork.

What a specification captures

Work is described in units the business would recognise — a capability, not one entry per underlying technical component (mirroring the components one-for-one would produce thousands of meaningless items). Each specification captures four things: the information involved, the rules and automations behind it, the screens and behaviour people see, and — above all — the what and the why. The why is what stops the right thing being built the wrong way, or the wrong thing being built well.

The how — the technical design — is a separate step, taken only after the what and why are accepted. The business owns the outcome; the technical team owns the design that meets it. They are separated by a gate, not filled in on one form by everyone at once.

Toward a living, visual catalogue

Today the picture of how the business works lives largely in a few people's heads, and new joiners learn by trial and error. The ambition is a living, visual catalogue of the end-to-end processes — current and target — kept current by the way of working rather than by a manual refresh, and clear enough to onboard from.

Two things are needed and should not be confused: a tool to draw the process flows, and a shared drawing standard so everyone maps a process the same way. Both are still to be settled — a short assessment of the available tools comes first, ahead of the mapping work. Crucially, these processes cross both business areas and system boundaries: a single process can touch several of the systems around the recruitment platform, so the maps have to be drawn in one shared space rather than one per team, and capturing them needs access to those neighbouring systems.

Still open. The method itself is proposed, pending confirmation at the governance forum. Still to settle: the tooling and the shared drawing standard for the process catalogue, and access to the neighbouring systems whose steps form part of each end-to-end process.
